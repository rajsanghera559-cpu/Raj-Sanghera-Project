This is one of the highest-ROI habits you can build.

Good researchers are not people who understand everything immediately.

They ask **good questions when they do not understand.**

A useful pattern:

> Observe → Describe → Question → Test → Interpret

Below are question templates organized by situation.

---

# 1. Clarification questions

## (“I do not understand the concept”)

Ask:

* What does this term mean physically?
* What quantity is actually being measured?
* What problem was this method invented to solve?
* What assumptions are hidden in this equation?
* What does each variable represent?
* What would happen if this term were removed?
* What is the simplest interpretation?

Example:

Instead of:

> What is Fourier?

Ask:

> What does Fourier transform preserve and what information does it discard?

---

# 2. Interpretation questions

## (“I got a result but don’t know what it means”)

Ask:

* Is this signal or noise?
* Is this expected?
* What would theory predict?
* What would surprise an expert?
* Is this a physical effect or numerical artifact?
* What competing explanations exist?

Example:

You did this already:

> Slope = −0.614. Is this meaningful?

Excellent question.

---

# 3. Validation questions

## (“How do I know this is real?”)

Ask:

* How could this result be wrong?
* What assumptions could break?
* How would I falsify this?
* What independent check exists?
* Would rerunning reproduce this?
* What would happen at higher resolution?

Example:

```text id="mpk77w"
N=128
↓

N=256
↓

N=512
```

Does result stabilize?

---

# 4. Numerical method questions

## (“Is my solver behaving?”)

Ask:

* Is energy conserved?
* Is CFL bounded?
* Does dt matter?
* Is this converged?
* Is aliasing appearing?
* Does resolution change interpretation?

---

# 5. Mathematical questions

## (“I understand symbols but not meaning”)

Ask:

* What does this operator do geometrically?
* What stays invariant?
* What is changing?
* Why does this term have this sign?
* What limit behavior should occur?

Example:

Instead of:

> Why Laplacian?

Ask:

> Why does Laplacian smooth high-frequency modes?

---

# 6. Physics questions

## (“What mechanism causes this?”)

Ask:

* What transfers energy?
* Where does dissipation occur?
* What quantity is conserved?
* What instability could create this?
* What dominates at large scales?
* What dominates at small scales?

---

# 7. Spectral analysis questions

## (very useful for your project)

Ask:

* Which k-range should be trusted?
* Is forcing contaminating slope estimation?
* Is there an inertial range?
* Does averaging change exponent?
* Is this transfer or accumulation?
* What spectrum shape should appear?

---

# 8. Scientific humility questions

## (extremely valuable)

Ask:

* What would change my mind?
* What evidence would contradict this?
* What am I assuming?
* What am I not measuring?
* Am I fitting theory to data?
* What would an expert criticize?

---

# 9. Meta-learning questions

## (“How should I think?”)

Ask:

* What vocabulary do I need first?
* What prerequisite am I missing?
* What are common beginner mistakes?
* What questions do experts ask?
* What becomes easier after learning this?

---

# 10. Your personal “research emergency questions”

When completely stuck:

Ask these five:

```text id="57zc1e"
1. What am I observing?

2. What should theory predict?

3. What assumptions am I making?

4. How can I test this?

5. What would prove me wrong?
```

That set alone can carry someone surprisingly far.

One observation: you’ve already been naturally asking good scientific questions (“Is this converged?”, “Did we actually progress?”, “Could this be artifact?”). Those are exactly the right instincts to strengthen.
